BearyChat brought team conversations and updates from connected services into one workspace. As Product Manager, I worked on product strategy, requirements, and UX, managed the design team, and coordinated development during its transition from a free service to a commercial product.

## Context and Challenge

The product needed to serve Chinese teams, give growing organizations a reason to pay, and make an expanding feature set easier to navigate. Bots were an important part of the experience, but most delivered information without helping people act on it.

Before joining, I worked with the team to establish a guiding design principle:

Teamwork runs on information: conversations, updates from connected tools, and shared documents. BearyChat helps teams organize that information and turn it into useful context.

That principle shaped three priorities:

1. Design a paid product suited to the Chinese market.
2. Make bots more useful within conversations.
3. Bring the growing feature set into a coherent experience.

## Give the Team a Shared Design Language

When I joined, BearyChat had 15 employees across Beijing, Shenzhen, and remote locations. Designers and engineers needed a common way to describe how an interaction should work.

I introduced a vocabulary for UX flows, drawing on UML and my previous experience. The flowchart template distinguished interfaces, system actions, decision points, and linked subflows.

## Design Around How Chinese Teams Worked

Although BearyChat shared many core features with Slack, its interactions needed to reflect the teams using it.

Reminders were one example. We provided a visual interface instead of requiring people to enter commands. Teams could share recurring reminders, supporting a common schedule without requiring everyone to use a separate calendar.

## Build a Commercial Offering

BearyChat had been free for its first year. I worked on the pricing and product strategy, using comparisons with similar products and interviews with medium and large teams to define the plans.

### Keep the Free Plan Useful

We expanded the message history and bot allowance in the free plan. Small teams could continue using the product, while larger organizations could evaluate the additional capacity and controls in the paid offering.

### Connect Paid Features to Team Needs

The paid plan included full message history and more bots. Interviews also brought two requirements into focus:

- **Reliability:** bots carried business-critical updates. The plan increased bot capacity and included service-level agreements.
- **External collaboration:** guest accounts needed access to selected conversations, with controls over channel access and message history.

For enterprises that needed to keep information within their own systems, we designed a separate plan with private deployment and deeper customization.

### Make Customization Part of the Product Structure

The enterprise plan was central to the revenue strategy. Modular code and interface design let customers combine the capabilities they needed. We applied this approach to customization work for Huawei and Manila Group.

## Reorganize a Growing Workspace

Features arrived through customer feedback, usage data, and internal ideas. Each addressed a need, but grouping them by similarity made the overall workspace harder to understand.

We redesigned the layout around the existing product and its near-term direction, separating navigation from the conversation itself:

1. Hierarchical navigation organized features into business-focused modules, supporting both everyday use and customer-specific configurations.
2. The content area kept the conversation and message input central. Files, saved information, and contacts stayed connected to the conversations that gave them context.

## Extend Bots from Updates to Actions

Earlier bots mostly forwarded updates and sent people to another service to act. The “Interactive Robots” plan explored actions within the conversation:

- The short-term plan introduced interactive inputs, buttons, and immediate feedback within bot messages.
- The longer-term plan proposed a marketplace where people could share bots for free or offer paid integrations.

These were roadmap directions. The marketplace proposal should be read as a planned extension of the product.

## Outcome and Learning

I helped guide BearyChat's move from a free service to a commercial product, connecting its pricing, team workflows, and interface structure. The work included shared requirements tools, reminder interactions, enterprise customization, and a workspace redesign.

The case shows how product strategy and interaction design informed each other. Modular features supported customization; the growing product also needed an information architecture organized around the way teams worked.
